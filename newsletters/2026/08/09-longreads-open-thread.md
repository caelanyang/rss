* Victor Niederhoffer died this week. Here's John Cassidy's New Yorker profile of him from 2007 . Niederhoffer was a squash champion, very risk-tolerant hedge fund manager, and collector of unusual art and unusual friends. A few people have gotten in trouble for how they phrased this, but: if you're memorializing Niederhoffer in any non-generic way, you simply can't avoid mentioning what a supremely weird dude he was.

> **译文：**维克多·尼德霍夫本周去世。以下是约翰·卡西迪2007年为《纽约客》撰写的关于他的人物特写。尼德霍夫是一位壁球冠军，一位风险承受能力极强的对冲基金经理，同时也是一位收藏奇特艺术品和结交奇特朋友的人。有些人因为措辞不当而惹上麻烦，但我想说的是：如果你要以任何非泛泛的方式纪念尼德霍夫，就无法回避提及他是个多么古怪的人。

Junto was an eclectic meetup, and he never had any trouble being the strangest person in the room—both in the sense that it was usually him, and that he was supremely unbothered by this. The article mentions NYC Junto, a monthly meetup he hosted that I used to attend occasionally.

> **译文：**Junto是一个包罗万象的聚会，而他总是能毫不费力地成为房间里最古怪的人——既因为他通常就是最古怪的人，也因为他对此毫不在意。文章中提到了纽约Junto，这是他每月举办一次的聚会，我以前偶尔会参加。

Junto was a weird survivor of the Big Sort: there were young finance and tech professionals, septuagenarian libertarians who could tell you about meeting Ayn Rand, or people who were attending because they liked the speaker and had no clue what they were getting into. The only rule was that any time an audience member had a question, the speaker was required to pause and answer them.

> **译文：**Junto 是“大洗牌”中一个奇特的幸存者：这里有年轻的金融和科技从业者，有能跟你讲述与安·兰德会面经历的七十多岁自由意志主义者，还有一些人只是因为喜欢演讲者而来，却对即将发生的一切一无所知。唯一的规则是，任何时候有听众提问，演讲者都必须停下来回答。

So once a month in midtown, you could basically participate in an improvised dramatic production called The Night the Comments Section Came to Life. Somehow, someone who'd had six kids by two wives and another on the side, who'd blown up multiple times at his own hedge fund and others, managed to keep this unstable-by-default arrangement going for decades. Nobody else could have pulled it off.

> **译文：**所以，每个月在市中心，你基本上都可以参与一场名为“评论区复活之夜”的即兴戏剧表演。不知怎的，一个娶了两个老婆，还有个情人，一共生了六个孩子，在自己的对冲基金和其他基金里都多次破产的人，竟然能让这种本身就不稳定的关系维持几十年。换作别人，根本不可能做到。

* Varun Godbole, who spent a decade working on deep learning, has a personal story about agonizing over the decision of whether or not to take an offer from an AI lab . This is lightly fictionalized, but keen observers of AI may be able to read between the lines and produce a good guess as to the identity of "Mario," who left OpenAI to start a more safety-focused lab called "Noetic." In one sense, the mental agony of deciding precisely which incredibly lucrative job to take is unsympathetic. On the other hand, the more of the world that gets mediated through AI, the more your life is affected by exactly this kind of thought process. It's nice to get a look at the thought process of the people who build the tools that do a growing share of the world's thinking.

> **译文：*** 曾从事深度学习十年的Varun Godbole讲述了他纠结于是否接受一家人工智能实验室的聘用邀请的个人经历。这个故事虽略带虚构成分，但敏锐的人工智能观察者或许能从字里行间猜出“Mario”的真实身份——他离开了OpenAI，创办了专注于安全性的实验室Noetic。从某种意义上说，在如此高薪的职位之间做出抉择的痛苦挣扎令人难以同情。但另一方面，随着人工智能在世界范围内扮演的角色越来越重要，你的生活也越来越受到这种思维方式的影响。能够了解那些构建工具的人们的思考过程，并探究这些工具如何承担起日益增长的全球思考任务，也是一件很有意义的事情。

* Substack convenes a panel where Kathleen Schmidt, John Paul Brammer, and Lincoln Michel discuss the story of a Call Me, I'll Hide the Body, a $2m book deal that fell apart over allegations that the author used AI to write it . What's striking about this one is that the author's agent was the one to make the call. There are plenty of places where anyone who feels like it can publish creative writing; you can serialize it on Substack, sell ebooks, self-publish, etc.

> **译文：**Substack 组织了一场座谈会，Kathleen Schmidt、John Paul Brammer 和 Lincoln Michel 讨论了《叫我，我会藏尸》（Call Me, I'll Hide the Body）一书的出版故事。这本书价值 200 万美元，但最终因作者被指控使用人工智能写作而告吹。令人震惊的是，最终取消出版合同的是作者的经纪人。如今，任何有志于发表创意作品的人都有很多平台可以发布；你可以在 Substack 上连载，也可以出售电子书、自行出版等等。

If you go through gatekeepers, the assumption from a buyer at the other end is that the process is gatekept. But it can be hard for gatekeepers to resist social or financial pressure—it was probably hard for the agent to write the email turning down both $300,000 and also missing the chance to represent a hot new author.

> **译文：**如果交易要经过中间人，买家就会认为整个过程都受到把关。但中间人很难抵挡社会或经济压力——对经纪人来说，拒绝30万美元的邀约，同时错失代理一位炙手可热的新作家的机会，想必也是件难事。

But it's the right call: it only gets easier to detect LLM use over time, so we'll all be judged based on the norms and evidence-gathering techniques of the future, not the present.

> **译文：**但这是正确的决定：随着时间的推移，检测 LLM 的使用只会变得越来越容易，所以我们都将根据未来的规范和证据收集技术来评判，而不是根据现在的规范和证据收集技术。

* Jasmine Sun visited a few towns where there's a lively debate about whether or not to build a datacenter . It's fun to read this as a story of completely out-of-touch rich people—they aren't rich financially, but they are in the sense that they can redirect resources towards their preferences.

> **译文：*** Jasmine Sun走访了一些小镇，那里正在热烈讨论是否要建设数据中心。把这看作是一群完全脱离实际的富人的故事，读起来很有趣——他们并非财力雄厚，而是能够随心所欲地支配资源。

If a tech billionaire bought a stretch of land specifically because it was the last undeveloped stretch of real estate between two cities and he liked that part of the drive, you might view this as conspicuous consumption, or a wildly inefficient form of philanthropy. But for someone opposed to datacenter development, it's perfectly natural to impose that preference on everyone else.

> **译文：**如果一位科技亿万富翁买下一块地，仅仅是因为它是两座城市之间最后一块未开发的土地，而且他喜欢沿途的风景，你可能会认为这是炫耀性消费，或者是一种效率极低的慈善行为。但对于反对数据中心开发的人来说，将这种偏好强加于他人却是再自然不过的事情了。

Another activist talks about a town that used to have a GM plant, and, since GM left, has been trying to redevelop it.

> **译文：**另一位活动人士谈到，一个曾经拥有通用汽车工厂的小镇，自从通用汽车撤离后，就一直在努力重新开发该镇。

Unfortunately, GM left behind some heavy metals and chemical contaminants in the spill, so nobody wanted it—until a datacenter came along and tried to buy it, at which point activists once again stepped in. "What if the project gets abandoned?" asks one of them. (The answer is that they'd be back where they started, except without the hazardous waste and with however many millions of dollars in taxes the datacenter paid.) The piece is rounded out with some pleasant talk about democracy, but given the arguments it presents, it's actually pretty critical: if you have a system that makes it easier to block things than to do them, you're achieving a kind of sad economic stasis that most people wouldn't vote for.

> **译文：**不幸的是，通用汽车泄漏事故留下了一些重金属和化学污染物，因此无人问津——直到一家数据中心出现并试图收购，这时环保人士再次介入。“如果项目被放弃怎么办？”其中一位环保人士问道。（答案是，他们又回到了原点，只不过没有了危险废物，而且数据中心还缴纳了数百万美元的税款。）文章最后还愉快地谈到了民主，但考虑到它提出的论点，实际上相当批判：如果你的制度让阻止事情比做事情更容易，那么你就会陷入一种令人沮丧的经济停滞状态，而大多数人不会投票支持这种状态。

Then again, there are a lot of places with cheap land and little economic activity, and some of them will probably be happy about the tax revenue.

> **译文：**话又说回来，有很多地方地价低廉，经济活动也不活跃，其中一些地方的居民可能会乐于接受税收收入。

* In Ars Technica, Alan Bradley remembers the Internet before search got good . In a way, we're returning to this: the way you'd find cool things online in the late 90s, as I faintly remember, went like this: somebody else found something cool, and they linked to it and told you why it was awesome. You'd follow a chain of such links and end up somewhere pretty weird—it was pretty common for personal sites to have a list such links, as a kind of collective effort to survey little bits of the web.

> **译文：**在 Ars Technica 上，Alan Bradley 回忆了搜索功能完善之前的互联网。某种程度上，我们又回到了那个时代：我依稀记得，在 90 年代末，人们在网上寻找好东西的方式是这样的：有人发现了一些好东西，然后贴上链接，告诉你它有多棒。你顺着这些链接一路追踪，最终会发现一些非常奇特的东西——个人网站上经常会列出这样的链接，这就像是一种集体探索网络世界的方式。

And then search got good at cooking up recommended links for topics nobody had bothered to explore themselves, and that turned into the default discovery mechanism when you know roughly what you want but not exactly where it is. But LLMs are shifting things back to a more personalized model, because, if you have memory on, they'll generate answers that are tilted towards and contextualized by the user's existing interests.

> **译文：**后来，搜索引擎擅长为一些无人问津的主题推荐链接，这逐渐成为用户大致知道自己想要什么但又不确定具体位置时的默认发现机制。但生命周期管理（LLM）正在将搜索模式拉回更加个性化的方向，因为如果你启用了记忆功能，它们会根据用户已有的兴趣生成更符合其情境的答案。

You'd get this organically if the way you found links was to visit the personal homepages of people you already knew and got along with, and now there's an even-more-similar-to-you source that's interested in whatever you want it to be.

> **译文：**如果你获取链接的方式是访问你已经认识并且相处融洽的人的个人主页，那么你自然而然就会获得这些链接。现在，又出现了一个与你更加相似的来源，它对你想要的任何东西都感兴趣。

* This week in Capital Gains, we talk about the question that comes up every time a hedge fund loses money on a directional bet: do we really have to call them 'hedge funds' if they don't necessarily hedge ? At this point, it's just a linguistic curiosity—except that the biggest hedge funds, who keep taking more share in the industry, are hedged to a degree that would astound Alfred Winslow Jones. (This also features a fun bit of finance trivia: Ben Graham, father of value investing and operator of an early hedge fund, actually put a little money into American Research & Development, which is usually viewed as the first VC fund. It's a small world.)

> **译文：*** 本周的《资本收益》节目中，我们将探讨一个每次对冲基金在方向性押注中亏损时都会出现的问题：如果它们未必进行对冲，我们真的还要称它们为“对冲基金”吗？目前，这只是一个语言上的趣味问题——只不过，那些在行业中不断占据更大份额的大型对冲基金，其对冲程度之高，足以令阿尔弗雷德·温斯洛·琼斯（Alfred Winslow Jones）瞠目结舌。（这里还有一个有趣的金融小知识：价值投资之父、早期对冲基金的运营者本杰明·格雷厄姆（Ben Graham）实际上曾向美国研发公司（American Research & Development）投入过少量资金，而该公司通常被认为是第一家风险投资基金。世界真小。）

* A Read. Haus user asks if the rise of multi-manager shops with tight risk mandates means that we'll see more market crashes that are invisible in overall equity indices —in other words, a crash where, say, half of the oil companies are up 10%, half are down 10%, but energy stocks overall are pretty flat.

> **译文：*** 阅读。Haus 用户询问，随着风险控制严格的多经理机构的兴起，是否意味着我们将看到更多在整体股票指数中无法体现的市场崩盘——换句话说，例如，一半的石油公司上涨 10%，一半下跌 10%，但能源股整体表现却相当平稳。

There are small moves like this all the time—the running joke on fintwit is that whenever a bunch of stocks in the same industry are behaving inexplicably, you say "hearing a pod blew up." And some of the time, it's probably true—once, in my investment research days, I saw a particular stock just keep dribbling down all day, ending the day -5% on no news. So I emailed a buy-side client who I knew was very close to the company, and very bullish, to ask what was going on.

> **译文：**这类小幅波动时有发生——金融圈里有个梗，每当同一行业的几只股票出现莫名其妙的走势时，你就说这是“听障人士的播客爆了”。有时候，这话可能还真没错——我以前做投资研究的时候，就遇到过一只股票整天都在小幅下跌，最后在没有任何消息的情况下收跌了5%。于是我给一位跟这家公司关系密切、而且非常看好的买方客户发了邮件，问问到底是怎么回事。

The email bounced, and there was my answer: he’d gotten fired, and his portfolio was unceremoniously liquidated. But big moves are less plausible: the pod shops all know that this dynamic exists, and they vary in their preference for cutting losses or taking on some directional risk in order to exploit the variance. Meanwhile, companies can react, by stepping up their buyback or quickly issuing more stock.

> **译文：**邮件被退回，我的回复是：他被解雇了，他的投资组合也被毫不留情地清算了。但大刀阔斧的改革不太可能发生：所有参与投资组合的公司都明白这种动态的存在，它们在止损或承担一定方向性风险以利用市场波动方面各有偏好。与此同时，公司可以通过加大股票回购力度或迅速增发股票来做出反应。

And long-only investors, or even longer-horizon hedge funds, will also tend to take advantage of these dislocations. A crash happens when liquidity is suddenly scarce for everyone. If it's suddenly scarce for just one fund, that's too bad for them but probably not something the rest of us need to worry about.

> **译文：**而那些只做多的投资者，甚至是投资期限更长的对冲基金，也往往会利用这些市场错位。当流动性突然对所有人来说都变得稀缺时，就会发生崩盘。如果流动性突然变得稀缺，那对它们来说当然很糟糕，但对我们其他人来说可能并不是什么值得担忧的事情。

Seveneves : My seven-year-old has gotten in the habit of asking my elaborate hypothetical questions involving complicated feats of engineering, space, and unusual ways for people to die. Usually, all I can do here is offer a little technical guidance (e.g. "coating the cruise missile in a powerful neurotoxin probably wouldn't kill more of the people you're launching it at, but it might kill the people who were trying to launch it.") But one time he asked me what would happen if the moon exploded, and, fortunately, I had some detailed reference material on exactly that. (As with Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman , I recommend reading ahead and censoring some details and some lengthy passages. At a few points you'll zip through several pages with "And then all of the people died in various horrible ways.")

> **译文：**Seveneves：我七岁的儿子最近养成了一个习惯，喜欢问我一些复杂的假设性问题，涉及复杂的工程技术、太空探索以及各种奇特的死法。通常，我只能提供一些技术指导（比如，“在巡航导弹上涂上强效神经毒素可能不会杀死更多目标，但可能会杀死发射导弹的人。”）。但有一次，他问我如果月球爆炸会发生什么，幸运的是，我手头正好有一些关于这方面的详细参考资料。（就像《费曼先生，您肯定是在开玩笑吧》一样，我建议提前阅读并删减一些细节和冗长的段落。因为有些地方你会快速翻阅好几页，上面都是“然后所有人都以各种可怕的方式死去。”）

The basic setup is exactly that: some unknown force splits the moon into seven rocks, these rocks start colliding with one another, that process accelerates, and it quickly becomes apparent that in about two years, the earth will be incinerated by a cloud of meteors, which will stick around for thousands of years.

> **译文：**基本设定就是这样：某种未知的力量将月球分裂成七块岩石，这些岩石开始相互碰撞，这个过程不断加速，很快人们就会发现，大约两年后，地球将被一团流星云焚毁，而这团流星云将持续数千年。

It's very much a book written for nerds, both because it has lots of detailed descriptions of clever ways one might try to stay alive in space and because the good guys tend to be fighter pilots-turned-astronauts, roboticists, pop scientists with sterling academic credentials, etc., while the bad guys tend to be politicians with Dickensian names. If a character ever gets themselves out of a tight spot by writing a Python script, you know what kind of reader this book is meant to appeal to.

> **译文：**这本书简直就是为书呆子写的，不仅因为它详细描述了在太空中生存的各种巧妙方法，还因为书中的正面人物往往是战斗机飞行员出身的宇航员、机器人专家、拥有卓越学术背景的科普科学家等等，而反派则往往是名字像狄更斯笔下人物一样的政客。如果某个角色靠编写Python脚本脱困，你就知道这本书的目标读者是哪一类了。

In some ways, this book is a time capsule of fifteen years ago: there's a character who is a loving parody of Elon Musk: he calls himself as an "Asp-hole," is impossible to manage, and—so it doesn't pick up every beat—he doesn't tell everybody what he's planning but does get things done. And it was written when a global mass-casualty event was a hypothetical, rather than a reality, so it imagines that most of us would react with some combination of calmly going about our lives or spending all of our waking hours helping to avert disaster at great personal cost. There's some artistic license there, for sure.

> **译文：**从某种程度上说，这本书就像十五年前的时光胶囊：书中有一个角色是对埃隆·马斯克的戏仿，他自称“混蛋”，难以驾驭，而且——虽然书中并非完全还原当时的情景——他不会把计划告诉所有人，但却总能把事情办成。这本书写于全球大规模伤亡事件还只是假设而非现实的年代，因此书中设想，大多数人的反应要么是冷静地继续生活，要么是竭尽全力、不计个人得失地去阻止灾难。当然，这其中肯定有一些艺术加工。

The story starts deliberately anchored to modern technology, basically asking how humanity survives the end of the world with mid-2010s tech, and with various strains of mid-2010s nerd culture.

> **译文：**故事一开始就刻意以现代科技为背景，探讨人类如何在 2010 年代中期的科技水平和各种 2010 年代中期的宅文化影响下，在世界末日中生存下来。

Over time, the book necessarily has to make more and more imaginative assumptions about technology, culture, and the interplay between them (there's a fun little riff about a society where consumer electronics use cheap, lagging-edge chips, because all the good stuff obviously needs to be saved for robots). So it eventually evolves into a sword & sorcery & spaceships, albeit with more discussions of how one might implement magical-seeming tools, vehicles, etc.

> **译文：**随着剧情发展，这本书必然要对科技、文化以及二者之间的相互作用做出越来越天马行空的假设（书中有一个有趣的小插曲：在一个消费电子产品都使用廉价落后芯片的社会里，因为所有好东西显然都要留给机器人）。最终，它演变成了一部剑与魔法、太空飞船的奇幻小说，只不过其中更多地探讨了如何实现那些看似神奇的工具、载具等等。

Which is still fun, but a different genre: unless you're deliberately setting a science fiction book entirely in a short span around the present, it naturally drifts into fantasy. But the readership of these genres has substantial overlap, so putting both genres into the same book works reasonably well.

> **译文：**这仍然很有趣，但却是不同的类型：除非你刻意将科幻小说的全部故事背景设定在当代前后一段短暂的时间里，否则它自然而然就会滑向奇幻领域。不过，这两种类型的读者群体有很大的重叠，所以将两种类型融合在同一本书中效果还不错。

The archetype the book celebrates is the non-performative survivalist. The book features miners, nuclear submarine captains, and of course astronauts, all of whom are expected to exhaustively plan for every possible contingency and also to be ready to improvise when there's inevitably some problem they didn't think of.

> **译文：**这本书颂扬的是一种不事张扬的生存主义者原型。书中描绘了矿工、核潜艇艇长，当然还有宇航员，他们都被要求为每一种可能发生的突发情况制定详尽的计划，同时也要随时准备应对那些他们始料未及的问题。

In the real world, these people get periodically praised when they do something visibly heroic—landing a plane on the Hudson River ; refusing to launch a retaliatory nuclear strike on America without getting confirmation that the equipment isn't acting up ; ignoring instructions to remain in place on 9/11, evacuating thousands of people, and then dying while trying to get more of them out ; or, thematically, coming up with a way to jury-rig a modified CO2 scrubber on the ground and then implementing it in space .

> **译文：**在现实世界中，这些人会定期受到表扬，因为他们做出了一些显而易见的英勇行为——比如在哈德逊河上迫降飞机；在确认设备没有故障之前拒绝向美国发动报复性核打击；在9/11事件中无视留在原地的指令，疏散了数千人，然后在试图救出更多人时牺牲；或者，从主题上看，想出一种方法在地面上临时搭建一个改进的二氧化碳吸收器，然后将其应用到太空中。

But most of these people spend most of their lives making sure things don't go wrong rather than dealing with it when they do. But the fact that light switches, faucets, seatbelts, and supply chains all do most of what they're supposed to do most of the time relies on this sort of person, and it's nice to wrap a hymn to them around a pretty fun story.

> **译文：**但这些人中的大多数一生都在忙于确保事情不出错，而不是在出错时去处理。然而，电灯开关、水龙头、安全带和供应链之所以能在大多数情况下正常运转，正是依赖于这类人，用一个相当有趣的故事来赞美他们，也是一件很美好的事情。
