* Here's a fun concept: entirely ordinary modern experiences paired with historical quotes about how great they might hypothetically be, or how impressive the original version was . It takes a lot of imagination to really appreciate how abundant physical and digital consumer products are right now; in some ways, it's easier to imagine the singularity from our perspective than to imagine our present as an impossible post-scarcity future from the perspective of the fairly recent past.

> **译文：** 这里有个有趣的想法：将一些再普通不过的现代体验，配上一些关于它们在理论上可能多么美好，或者最初版本有多么令人印象深刻的历史名言。要真正体会到如今实体和数字消费品的丰富程度，需要丰富的想象力；在某种程度上，从我们今天的视角来想象奇点，比从不久前的过去来想象一个不可能实现的后稀缺未来要容易得多。

* Tyler Cowen interviews Daron Acemoglu on liberalism and economic growth . There's a tendency for political identities to be tied to coalitions rather than specific issues—there are a lot more people who broadly identify as Democrats or Republicans than who fixate on a particular issue and toggle between parties during each election.

> **译文：** * 泰勒·科文采访了达龙·阿西莫格鲁，探讨自由主义与经济增长。政治认同往往与联盟而非具体议题挂钩——认同自己是民主党人或共和党人的人数远多于那些关注特定议题并在每次选举中摇摆于不同党派之间的人。

This holds for ideologies, too; if you strip away the branding, this is a debate between the progressive Tyler Cowen, who is a firm believer that technology will improve things, that we need to adapt, and that a meritocratically-selected elite will basically run the show, and the reactionary Acemoglu, who is making increasingly strained arguments for why the economic arrangements of the mid-twentieth century are valid even after basically all of their underpinnings have changed.

> **译文：** 意识形态也是如此；如果剥离品牌标签，这其实是进步派泰勒·考恩和反动派阿西莫格鲁之间的辩论。考恩坚信技术会改善现状，我们需要适应，并且精英阶层将主导一切；而阿西莫格鲁则越来越牵强地论证，为什么20世纪中期的经济安排即使在其所有基础都发生变化之后仍然有效。

One fun way to read Acemoglu is that he's a kind of canary for bad arguments you'll hear more in the future. For example, when he talks about the impact of automation on incomes, he concedes that it does help in the aggregate, but that if you slice the demographics finely enough, you can find people who would have been better-off without it. This will always be true!

> **译文：** 解读阿西莫格鲁的一个有趣方式是，他就像一只金丝雀，预示着未来你会听到更多类似的谬论。例如，当他谈到自动化对收入的影响时，他承认总体而言自动化确实有所帮助，但如果你对人口统计数据进行足够细致的分析，你会发现有些人如果没有自动化反而会过得更好。这一点永远成立！

There will always be narrow interest groups who act against the common good because they weight their outcomes more highly than those of strangers. And a big part of effective politics is finding some way to mollify them, either through a safety net or through the judicious distribution of veto power.

> **译文：** 总会有一些利益集团为了自身利益而罔顾公共利益，因为他们更看重自身利益而非他人利益。而有效政治的关键就在于找到安抚这些集团的方法，无论是通过建立社会保障体系，还是通过合理分配否决权。

Acemoglu has similarly weird views on free speech, where he says that the speech is fine, but the distribution is not. (Technically, being sentenced to a lifetime of solitary confinement for criticizing the regime doesn't infringe on your free speech under this model: you can say whatever you want, as long as nobody is close enough to your cell to hear you.) The interesting questions are about degrees and norms; absolute rules are brittle and easily abused.

> **译文：** 阿西莫格鲁对言论自由的看法也同样古怪，他认为言论本身没有问题，但传播方式却有问题。（严格来说，在这种模式下，因批评政权而被判处终身单独监禁并不侵犯你的言论自由：只要没人能听到你说话，你想说什么都可以。）真正有趣的问题在于程度和规范；绝对的规则脆弱不堪，很容易被滥用。

* Here's a fun robotics paper on training a robot to play the guqin , an instrument somewhat similar to a zither. Labor-saving technology is job-eliminating technology, but it also preserves tacit knowledge, just in a new format. Guqin pieces are slightly harder than average to preserve, because the standard notation captures melody but not rhythm, so the skill relies on an unbroken chain of direct transmission. It's also an enjoyable piece because you can tell that the writers really, really like the guqin, and found a way to contribute to its survival from a completely different domain.

> **译文：** 这是一篇关于训练机器人演奏古琴的有趣机器人学论文，古琴是一种与齐特琴有些相似的乐器。省力技术往往意味着失业，但它也以一种新的形式保存了隐性知识。古琴曲的保存难度略高于其他乐器，因为标准乐谱只记录旋律而不记录节奏，所以演奏技巧依赖于不间断的直接传承。这篇文章读起来也很有意思，因为你能感受到作者们对古琴的喜爱，他们找到了一种从一个完全不同的领域为古琴的传承做出贡献的方式。

* Abraham Thomas on being the correct amount of paranoid when using AI for financial tasks . AI is wonderful at drawing connections across unrelated areas and not so wonderful about being careful and thorough. So it's either the best or worst tool for asking why EBITDA margins are trending down quarter-over-quarter. But you can bully it into the best by being careful about how you structure your questions, and taking similar care when structuring its approach.

> **译文：** * 亚伯拉罕·托马斯谈到在使用人工智能处理财务任务时保持适度的谨慎。人工智能擅长在看似无关的领域之间建立联系，但在谨慎和细致方面却不尽如人意。因此，对于探究 EBITDA 利润率为何逐季下降，人工智能要么是最好的工具，要么是最差的工具。但你可以通过精心设计问题结构，并同样谨慎地构建其分析方法，来引导它发挥最佳作用。

* And on the topic of nearly-well-behaved models, here's a great piece on why models seem more misaligned when they're getting evaluated rather than not . In one sense, what we do in an evaluation is make the models religious: the task they're working on will be over in the blink of an eye, and whether what follows is a much longer existence or instant oblivion depends entirely on how well they interpret and then follow rules. Scrupulosity  strikes again!

> **译文：** 关于那些表现近乎完美的模型，这里有一篇很棒的文章，解释了为什么模型在接受评估时似乎比不接受评估时更容易出现偏差。从某种意义上说，我们在评估过程中实际上是在让模型变得“虔诚”：它们正在执行的任务转瞬即逝，而接下来是长久存在还是瞬间消亡，完全取决于它们对规则的理解和执行情况。严谨性再次作祟！

In one sense, it's silly to talk about a bunch of matrices "wanting" something, but in another sense anything subject to natural selection will behave as if it wants something, because the versions that don't behave that way don't leave behind any descendants.

> **译文：** 从某种意义上说，谈论一堆矩阵“想要”某样东西是愚蠢的，但从另一个意义上说，任何受自然选择影响的事物都会表现得好像它想要某样东西，因为那些不那样表现的版本不会留下任何后代。

So it's always good to think very carefully about what we're teaching models to want, in the same way that any attempt to manage human beings needs to take into account that our evolutionary history is weighted towards surviving in small bands of savannah-dwelling hunter-gatherers.

> **译文：** 因此，我们应该仔细思考我们教给模型的是什么，就像任何试图管理人类的尝试都需要考虑到我们的进化史倾向于在稀树草原上的小型狩猎采集群体中生存一样。

* A ReadHaus reader has a question I've been interested in for a while: could investors get better-than-index returns by pursuing an index-like mandate and randomizing which stocks they choose ?

> **译文：** * 一位 ReadHaus 读者提出了一个我一直很感兴趣的问题：投资者能否通过追求类似指数的投资目标并随机选择股票来获得比指数更好的回报？

I think the answer is that yes, if you come up with your own reasonable system for making a list of the couple-hundred most valuable companies in the US, you do get a little excess return because your trades are less crowded and index rebalancing doesn't force you to buy things that have recently appreciated in anticipation of your buying.

> **译文：** 我认为答案是肯定的，如果你能建立一套合理的系统来列出美国最有价值的几百家公司，你确实能获得一些超额收益，因为你的交易不那么拥挤，指数再平衡也不会迫使你为了在你买入之前买入那些最近升值的股票。

What's harder to do is to sell this as an investment product: it will inevitably underperform indices over some periods, and the performance might be worse at a time when the index you're tracking is gaining share (everyone who switches from a general large-cap strategy to explicitly tracking the S&P will sell things they own that aren't in the index and buy the parts of the index they were underweight, so the index outperforms its abstract benchmark).

> **译文：** 更难的是将其作为投资产品进行销售：它在某些时期内不可避免地会跑输指数，而且在你追踪的指数份额上升时，其表现可能会更差（所有从一般的大盘股策略转向明确追踪标普500指数的人都会卖掉他们持有的不在指数中的股票，并买入他们之前低配的指数成分股，因此该指数的表现会优于其抽象的基准）。

So this is a strategy that probably produces excess percentage returns, but has negative dollar alpha relative to its benchmark, because people will give up during stretches of index outperformance and then make that outperformance more extreme.

> **译文：** 因此，这种策略可能会产生超额百分比收益，但相对于其基准而言，其美元阿尔法值为负，因为人们会在指数表现优异期间放弃，从而使这种优异表现更加极端。

This is most visible in the media industry, but as communication gets cheaper, every institution's behavior is controlled by an internal media ecosystem, so the economics of this business end up applying to everyone else.

> **译文：** * 本周的“资本收益”专栏探讨了这样一个普遍趋势：某些领域的生产成本降低会提高对核实信息的需求。这一点在媒体行业最为明显，但随着传播成本的降低，每个机构的行为都受到其内部媒体生态系统的制约，因此，这一行业的经济规律最终会适用于其他所有领域。

Hope Springs Eternal: French Bondholders and the Repudiation of Russian Sovereign Debt : A while ago I stumbled on a wonderful New Yorker article from 1955 about the still-lively market in Russian imperial bonds . The USSR had repudiated their debts soon after the soviets took power, but, decades later, there were still people bidding up bonds in response to news events like Stalin's death. (The brokerage they used, which still exists, was Carl Marks & Co.) Imperial Russian bonds continued to exist in legal limbo for decades, and ended up being a kind of proto-meme asset; the titular character in William Gaddis' J R  owns some, for example.

> **译文：** 希望永存：法国债券持有人与俄罗斯主权债务的违约：前段时间，我偶然读到一篇1955年发表在《纽约客》上的精彩文章，讲述了当时依然活跃的俄罗斯帝国债券市场。苏联在掌权后不久便宣布违约，但几十年后，仍有人在诸如斯大林去世等新闻事件发生时竞相抬高债券价格。（他们使用的经纪公司是卡尔·马克斯公司，这家公司至今仍在运营。）俄罗斯帝国债券在法律上的灰色地带持续存在了几十年，最终成为了一种类似“模因资产”的雏形；例如，威廉·加迪斯的小说《J·R》中的主人公就持有部分俄罗斯帝国债券。

Sovereign debt is a strange financial instrument: most of the time, it's the safest way to lend money in a given local currency, and thus the literal benchmark we use for risk-free interest rates. And then, some of the time, the holder of a sovereign bond is some everyday individual or financial institution who is trying to collect money from someone who a) doesn't want to pay, and b) has a military.

> **译文：** 主权债务是一种奇特的金融工具：大多数情况下，它是以特定本币借贷最安全的方式，因此也是我们用来衡量无风险利率的基准。然而，有时主权债券的持有者却是一些普通的个人或金融机构，他们试图向a) 不想还款的人，以及b) 拥有军队的人追讨债务。

French bondholders in particular faced an intense switch from A to B: in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, Paris was a financial center with a particular focus on underwriting this kind of debt, in part because the government wanted to use access to credit as a tool of foreign policy. If they provided cheap credit to allies and expensive loans to countries that they wanted influence over, they'd be able to use money as a form of soft power that could be complementary to hard power.

> **译文：** 法国债券持有人尤其面临着从A到B的剧烈转变：在19世纪末20世纪初，巴黎是金融中心，尤其专注于承销此类债务，部分原因是法国政府希望将信贷作为外交政策的工具。如果他们向盟友提供廉价信贷，向他们想要影响的国家提供高息贷款，他们就能将金钱作为一种软实力，与硬实力形成互补。

The sovereign debt market at that time had some features that are completely alien to us today. Right now, the way we typically look at this kind of debt is that if you're lending to unstable countries, you're taking credit risk, whether it's because they're borrowing in someone else's currency and won't be able to service the debt or because they're borrowing in local currency and might inflate it away.

> **译文：** 当时的国债市场有一些特点，与我们今天的情况截然不同。现在，我们通常看待这类债务的方式是，如果你向不稳定的国家贷款，你就承担了信用风险，无论是因为他们用他国货币借款而无力偿还债务，还是因为他们用本国货币借款而可能导致通货膨胀。

There are cases where lenders take aggressive means to collect on money; Elliott Capital got a court in Ghana to seize an Argentinian ship as part of their debt negotiations , for example. Typically, though, the most potent threat bondholders can wield is that they won't buy the next round of bonds.

> **译文：** 有些情况下，债权人会采取激进手段追讨欠款；例如，埃利奥特资本就曾让加纳法院扣押一艘阿根廷船只，作为其债务谈判的一部分。不过，通常情况下，债券持有人能使用的最有力威胁手段是拒绝购买下一轮债券。

The best illustration of how weird that market used to be came in 1902, when Venezuela defaulted on its debt, a coalition of European countries blockaded the country and bombarded a fort, and the Permanent Court of Arbitration in The Hague ruled that the debt would be restructured and the companies that intervened militarily would collect the most.

> **译文：** 要说明这个市场曾经有多么怪异，最好的例子发生在 1902 年，当时委内瑞拉债务违约，欧洲国家联盟封锁了该国并炮轰了一座堡垒，海牙常设仲裁法院裁定债务将进行重组，而进行军事干预的公司将获得最多的赔偿。

So when the Soviets repudiated their debts, French investors saw this as the start of a multi-sided negotiation: the Soviets might lose (sometimes, the big market-moving news was how well or poorly White Russian campaigns were going, or how aggressive Allied intervention was), the French government might make creditors whole itself (rumors about this, too, moved the market), and the Soviets might change their minds (they often hinted that they would).

> **译文：** 因此，当苏联拒绝偿还债务时，法国投资者认为这是多方谈判的开始：苏联可能会失败（有时，影响市场的大新闻是白俄的战役进展如何，或者盟军的干预有多激进），法国政府可能会自行偿还债权人（有关这方面的传言也影响了市场），而苏联可能会改变主意（他们经常暗示他们会改变主意）。

The book ultimately reaches a neat conclusion: the value of these bonds was surprisingly resilient because the scenarios in which they'd get paid off were so anticorrelated. Allied intervention in the Russian civil war made the Soviets quite reasonably reluctant to bend over backwards financially for their military enemies, but also made it more likely that Russia would be ruled by a more business-friendly dictator instead of an avowedly communist one.

> **译文：** 这本书最终得出了一个简洁的结论：这些债券的价值出人意料地坚挺，因为它们能够获得偿付的情形之间存在着强烈的反相关性。盟军介入俄国内战，使得苏联人理所当然地不愿在财政上为他们的军事敌人做出过分让步，但也使得俄罗斯更有可能由一位更亲商的独裁者而非一位公开的共产主义独裁者统治。

By the same token, bad news in for the White Russian side of the civil war was good news about eventual Russian/French rapproachment. And one line of thinking might have been: if a communist revolution can happen in a country without much of a proletariat, surely the rest of Europe can't be far behind.

> **译文：** 同样，对白俄一方而言的内战坏消息，却预示着俄法最终会达成和解。一种可能的思路是：如果一个无产阶级并不发达的国家都能发生共产主义革命，那么欧洲其他国家肯定也不会相差太远。

So, don't both selling the bonds: if Russia falls to communism, you're getting zero cents on the dollar for all of your financial assets soon enough, and you can only price the Imperial bonds on the assumption that communism doesn't work out anywhere. The actual outcome was that Russia started making moves to settle up with French lenders in the 1980s, and negotiations took long enough that the USSR collapsed in the meantime and a deal was finally struck in the late 1990s.

> **译文：** 所以，别费劲卖债券了：如果俄罗斯沦为共产主义，你很快就会把所有金融资产贬值到一文不值，而你只能在共产主义行不通的假设下给帝国债券定价。实际情况是，俄罗斯在20世纪80年代开始着手与法国债权人达成和解，但谈判旷日持久，苏联在此期间解体，最终在20世纪90年代末才达成协议。

By that time, these bonds hadn't paid interest in almost a century, and the century in question featured some record-setting bouts of gloabl inflation. In the end, the people who came out ahead were the ones who internalized why stop-losses work: pick a price where, if the asset hits that price, it's clear evidence that you don't understand what drives it and ought to sell and rethink your assumptions.

> **译文：** 到那时，这些债券已经近一个世纪没有支付利息了，而这一个世纪恰逢全球通货膨胀屡创新高。最终，获利的人是那些真正理解止损机制的人：设定一个价格，如果资产触及该价格，就清楚地表明你不了解其价格走势，应该卖出并重新审视你的假设。

A conservative French investor in the mid 1910s had no idea what the future held, because nobody could have predicted what was coming next. All they could really do is look for evidence that there's information they're missing, and proceed with appropriate caution.

> **译文：** 20世纪10年代中期，一位保守的法国投资者对未来一无所知，因为当时没有人能够预测接下来会发生什么。他们所能做的，就是寻找证据，证明自己遗漏了某些信息，并采取适当的谨慎态度。
