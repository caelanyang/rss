There is, however, an older technology. Back in the day (the 1920s) it was 
called “pyramiding,” <https://bloom.bg/3Qo4WC8> and it worked like this:

然而，还有一种更古老的技术。在20世纪20年代，它被称为“金字塔式定价法”（pyramiding），<https://bloom.bg/3Qo4WC8>，其工作原理如下：

---

* You have a company, Company A. It’s worth $100. You own 100% of it. It 
needs to raise money. 
 * You sell 30% of Company A to outside shareholders for $30. 
 * Now you have raised $30, and you own 70% of the voting stock of the company.
 * You need more money. 
 * You plop your 70% stake into a new holding company, Company B. You own 100% 
of Company B, and Company B’s only asset is 70% of the voting stock of Company 
A ($70 worth). 
 * You sell 30% of Company B to outside shareholders for $21. 
 * Now you have raised $51, and you own 70% of Company B, which owns 70% of 
Company A. You own a 49% economic stake in the operating company (Company A). 
But Company B owns a majority of the votes in Company A, and you own a majority 
of the votes in Company B, so you still have control over Company A. 
 * You need more money, you plop your Company B shares into Company C, and you 
sell 30% of Company C for $14.70. Now you have $65.70, but you still control 
70% of Company C, which controls 70% of Company B, which controls 70% of 
Company A. You still have control despite only owning about one-third of the 
stock economically. 
 * Company D, etc. 
You can see why this technology has mostly been replaced by dual-class 
supervoting stock. This is sort of messy and irregular. It also requires 
raising money at lots of different holding companies, rather than at one main 
company, which creates a lot of paperwork and is bad for liquidity and for 
storytelling.

* 你拥有一家公司，A公司，价值100美元。你拥有100%的股份。A公司需要筹集资金。

* 你以30美元的价格将A公司30%的股份出售给外部股东。

* 现在你筹集了30美元，并拥有A公司70%的投票权股份。

* 你需要更多资金。

* 你将这70%的股份投入到一家新的控股公司，B公司。你拥有B公司100%的股份，而B公司唯一的资产是A公司70%的投票权股份（价值70美元）。

* 你以21美元的价格将B公司30%的股份出售给外部股东。

* 现在你筹集了51美元，并拥有B公司70%的股份，而B公司又拥有A公司70%的股份。

你拥有运营公司（A公司）49%的经济权益。

但B公司拥有A公司的多数投票权，而你也拥有B公司的多数投票权，所以你仍然控制着A公司。

* 你需要更多资金，于是你将B公司的股份投入C公司，然后以每股14.70美元的价格出售C公司30%的股份。现在你拥有65.70美元，但你仍然控制着C公司70%的股份，C公司又控制着B公司70%的股份，B公司又控制着A公司70%的股份。尽管你实际只拥有大约三分之一的股份，但你仍然拥有控制权。

* D公司等等。

你可以看出为什么这项技术大多已被双重股权结构（超级投票权股票）所取代。这种结构比较混乱且不规范。它还需要在许多不同的控股公司筹集资金，而不是在一家主要公司筹集，这会产生大量的文书工作，不利于流动性和公司形象的展现。

Why? Well, I mean, the short answer is that prediction markets are not a 
philosophical mechanism for achieving truth; they are a convenient consumer 
gambling platform. If you want to establish thetruth of some question like 
“when did Strategy first sell Bitcoin,” or “when did US troops first enter Iran 
<https://bloom.bg/3QGWkXn>” for that matter, you might reach a tentative 
conclusion and be willing to revisit it whenever new facts come to light. 
Perhaps in 20 years newly declassified information will reveal that actually US 
special operations troops conducted secret ground missions in Iran in mid-March 
2026. But that doesn’t work for Polymarket: Polymarket took some bets on “US 
forces enter Iran by March 31,” and it needed to resolve those bets in a timely 
fashion, and it hasn’t heard anything about any secret ground missions, so 
those bets resolved to “No.” If in 20 years you do archival research and find 
out that Polymarket’s resolution was wrong, and you go to Polymarket to inform 
them, they will stare at you and say “you are completely missing the point 
here.”

为什么？简而言之，预测市场并非探求真理的哲学机制；它们只是一个方便的消费者赌博平台。如果你想确定某个问题的真相，比如“Strategy何时首次出售比特币”，或者“美军何时首次进入伊朗”，你或许可以得出一个初步的结论，并随时准备在新事实出现时重新审视它。

也许20年后，新解密的信息会揭示，实际上美国特种作战部队在2026年3月中旬在伊朗执行了秘密地面任务。但这并不适用于Polymarket：Polymarket接受了关于“美军在3月31日之前进入伊朗”的投注，它需要及时解决这些投注，但它没有收到任何关于秘密地面任务的消息，因此这些投注的结果是“否”。如果20年后你进行档案研究，发现Polymarket的决议是错误的，

然后你去Polymarket告知他们，

For a while, appraisal arbitrage had the reputation of offering asymmetric 
upside. In many places, when a public company is acquired for cash, 
shareholders who don’t like the deal price can go to court and ask for more 
money. This is called “appraisal”: The court will take evidence, decide how 
much the company was worth, and then order the acquirer to pay the disgruntled 
shareholders the appraised fair value of the company. Traditionally, the 
disgruntled shareholders will argue that the acquirer got a sweetheart deal and 
underpaid for the company, while the acquirer will argue that actually the deal 
was bitterly negotiated and the price was fair. And so traditionally the court 
would either (1) rule in favor of the shareholders and give them more than the 
deal price or (2) rule against the shareholders and give them the deal price. 
Either they’d get extra money, or they wouldn’t. But they’d neverlose money: 
The worst realistic outcome was just getting the deal price anyway.

money if you lost. In Delaware, where most US public company mergers happen, 
things were even better, because the acquirer had to pay you interest, at an 
unusually high rate, until the lawsuit was resolved. If youlost the lawsuit, 
you got the deal price plus a nice fixed-income return; if you won you did even 
better. “It was a good steady fixed-income strategy with some big equity
