Longreads

 * John Psmith reviews Xenophon's Anabasis [https://www.thediff.co/r/d67da16a?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c], the completely insane story of a group of Greek mercenaries who, in 401 BC, managed to: got hired for some minor military expedition, find out (after they've traveled too far to turn back) that they've actually been hired to help Cyrus the Younger seize control of the Persian Empire, and win a military victory on his behalf. At which point they learned that Cyrus had died, which meant they were now unemployed and deep in enemy territory. This is many problems at once, but the one that everything else depends on is organizational: determining who's in charge and what they're in charge of. Which leads to some interesting outcomes: he is, in some sense, a general, but he can't exactly order around 10,000 heavily-armed mercenaries. So he gets their vote on the big choices (often with a bit of gerrymandering) and then gets permission to handle the details, or he'll use divination to determine the next action, but reroll if he doesn't get the results he wanted. Anabasis is timeless, and this particular review makes it timely again.
 * In The American Scholar, Jonathan Weiner writes about the nature of memory, particularly how he and his brother differ so much in the way they remember things [https://www.thediff.co/r/4fec3503?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c]. Having extremely detailed, specific memories that let you basically rewind to some point in the past sounds like a superpower, but the piece makes it also sound like it's basically adding an autoplaying-at-full-volume video feed to your inner experience; storing memories like that means not extracting and storing specific details. A very thoughtful piece on much more than the science behind variation in memory. (Via The Browser [https://www.thediff.co/r/a7986a62?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c].)
 * Brady Brickner-Wood in The New Yorker on men who lie about their height [https://www.thediff.co/r/004b7117?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c]. It's a little strange that height is the rare immutable characteristic that it's perfectly socially acceptable to be a bigot about. And it's also not uncommon for people's revealed preferences to be less bigoted than their stated ones, which is fairly unusual. Once it's widely-understood that people are lying to themselves, it gets harder to preserve a norm against lying to them, and so the equilibrium is that people debate just how much lying, in either direction, is socially acceptable. It's a bit like having weak norms against tardiness, which can be a big economic drag, and hard to fix [https://www.thediff.co/r/c33e7cc7?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c].
 * Jonathan Chait in The Atlantic on the rise of the neo-Brandeisians [https://www.thediff.co/r/7624fd80?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c]. The main problems I have with the neo-Brandesians are that they aren't very well-informed, don't have good models to interpret what they do know, and tend to be pretty dishonest and annoying. (See here [https://www.thediff.co/r/71008227?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c] for a Diff review of a specific work.) Chait has a fun, brutal look at the movement, which is interesting in a meta sense because it gives people a little more permission to start moving away from the neo-Brandesians. Highlights include reading some of their earlier writing, from the 90s, where the movement developed from the observation that Walmart was hard to compete with and would end up controlling everyone's economic interactions. This did not turn out to be true. But the funniest point he makes is that the key insight of neo-Brandesians is that you can break up more companies if you're willing to do so even when it makes prices higher and makes consumers worse-off, and that the movement finally started getting real power in 2022, when inflation was hitting generational highs. This is more bad luck than anything they actually affected, but still kind of funny. (To be fair, the neo-Brandesian argument is that making consumers worse-off by breaking up efficient monopolies today saves us from being exploited by those monopolies in the future. But to make that case, you need to make it very, very clear that you deeply understand why those companies behave the way they do today. That has not happened.)
 * Kat Rosenfield writes about being canceled in the early wave of cancel culture [https://www.thediff.co/r/327bcac4?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c]. This headline/byline combo made me think that the story was going to be exaggerated, because I think of Rosenfield as part of a cluster of writers who are open-minded and culture war-flavored enough that they'll always be making enemies, but also that they have plenty of publications that are looking for exactly what they have to offer. Imagine my surprise when I learned that that's a new career, and she used to be a young adult novelist before getting in trouble over something that was about 15% ridiculous and 85% deliberately fictionalized. With the benefit of hindsight, we can look at the young adult novel community in general as an instructively bizarre one, but it must have been bewildering and unpleasant to experience this when the whole situation was brand new.
 * In this week's Capital Gains, a slight divergence from the usual fare: you never appreciate the sense in which we're all operating at close to 100% of capacity until you can't quite do that [https://www.thediff.co/r/a342188e?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c], sparked by an exciting injury. (Surgery yesterday, went fine.) It's a good reminder that we get used to surprisingly high levels of economic output, at many different levels, and that snapping one link in a supply chain (or a tendon) drastically limits what can be done.
 * A Read.Haus reader asks under what circumstances we could get a glut of compute [https://www.thediff.co/r/1ca3cd47?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c]. The pithy answer is that the more this question is meant rhetorically, the more likely it is to happen. And in more detail: compute is a tricky product, because it's fungible with so many other things: it replaces lots of different kinds of human labor, enhances different kinds in different ways, but also, in the context where it's being used to build algorithms that improve efficiency for industrial processes and logistics, it's a substitute for energy. And in the context of generative ads, as long as there's some upside to using it, it the inference capacity owned by big ad platforms can stay at pretty close to full utilization. But, we could overestimate how much economic growth there will be, how much room there is to optimize—what if the first trillion dollars actually does help us exhaustively identify every possible product or service, the next trillion perfectly optimizes the creation and distribution thereof, and the third trillion is a waste? But because of this extreme fungibility, it will be more fruitful to look at which individual companies will run into problems. We've already seen some, like xAI, buy more compute than they could directly use, and have to sell some of it to other companies. For now, that's put a floor on returns. But that's something to underwrite, not to assume.

约翰·普史密斯评论了色诺芬的《远征记》[https://www.thediff.co/r/d67da16a?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c]，讲述了一群希腊雇佣兵在公元前401年经历的荒诞故事：他们受雇执行一项小型军事远征，却在行军太远无法回头后才发现，他们实际上是受雇帮助小居鲁士夺取波斯帝国的统治权，并代表他取得了一场军事胜利。然而，就在此时，他们得知居鲁士已经去世，这意味着他们现在失业了，而且身处敌境深处。这引发了诸多问题，但所有问题的关键在于组织：确定谁负责以及他们负责什么。这导致了一些有趣的后果：从某种意义上说，他是一位将军，但他无法直接指挥一万名全副武装的雇佣兵。因此，他会让他们投票决定重大决策（通常会进行一些不公正的选区划分），然后获得处理细节的许可；或者，他会使用占卜来决定下一步行动，但如果结果不尽如人意，他会重新掷骰子。《远征记》经久不衰，而这篇评论又让它再次焕发了时代气息。

* 在《美国学者》一书中，乔纳森·韦纳探讨了记忆的本质，特别是他和他的兄弟在记忆方式上的巨大差异[https://www.thediff.co/r/4fec3503?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c]。拥有极其详细、具体的记忆，让你能够回到过去的某个时刻，这听起来像是一种超能力，但这篇文章却把它描述成像是给你的内心体验添加了一个自动播放且音量全开的视频流；这样存储记忆意味着无法提取和存储具体的细节。这是一篇发人深省的文章，探讨的内容远不止记忆差异背后的科学原理。（来源：The Browser [https://www.thediff.co/r/a7986a62?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c]）

* Brady Brickner-Wood 在《纽约客》上发表了一篇关于谎报身高的男性的文章 [https://www.thediff.co/r/004b7117?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c]。身高作为一种罕见的不可改变的特征，竟然能被社会完全接受地歧视，这有点奇怪。而且，人们实际表现出的偏好往往比他们公开宣称的偏好要温和得多，这本身就相当不寻常。一旦人们普遍意识到自己在自欺欺人，就很难维持禁止自欺欺人的规范，因此最终的平衡状态是，人们会争论究竟多少谎言（无论正反）在社会上是可以接受的。这有点像对迟到缺乏有效的规范，迟到会造成巨大的经济损失，而且很难纠正[https://www.thediff.co/r/c33e7cc7?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c]。

* 乔纳森·柴特在《大西洋月刊》上发表了关于新布兰代斯主义者兴起的文章[https://www.thediff.co/r/7624fd80?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c]。我对新布兰代斯主义者的主要不满在于他们信息匮乏，缺乏有效的模型来解读他们已知的知识，而且往往相当不诚实，令人厌烦。（参见此处[https://www.thediff.co/r/71008227?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c]，了解《The Diff》对某部作品的评论。）柴特以一种有趣而犀利的视角审视了这一运动，从某种意义上说，这很有意思，因为它让人们更有理由开始远离新布兰代斯主义者。亮点包括阅读他们90年代的一些早期文章，当时这场运动源于一个观察：沃尔玛难以匹敌，最终会控制所有人的经济互动。事实证明并非如此。但他提出的最有趣的观点是，新布兰德斯主义者的核心洞见在于，只要你愿意，即使这会导致物价上涨、消费者境况恶化，你也能拆分更多公司。而这场运动最终在2022年开始真正发力，当时通货膨胀率达到了历史新高。这更多是运气不佳而非他们实际影响的结果，但仍然颇具讽刺意味。 （平心而论，新布兰德斯主义的论点是，今天通过打破高效垄断企业让消费者境况恶化，是为了避免将来被这些垄断企业剥削。但要论证这一点，你必须非常非常清楚地表明你深刻理解这些公司今天行为背后的原因。而这一点并没有做到。）

* 卡特·罗森菲尔德（Kat Rosenfield）撰文讲述了她在早期“取消文化”浪潮中被“取消”的经历[https://www.thediff.co/r/327bcac4?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c]。这个标题/署名组合让我觉得这篇文章可能有些夸张，因为我认为罗森菲尔德属于这样一群思想开放、带有文化战争色彩的作家，他们注定会树敌，但同时也有很多媒体正需要他们所提供的内容。得知这竟然是她的新职业，而她之前是一位青少年小说作家，后来因为一部大约15%荒诞、85%纯属虚构的作品惹上麻烦，我真是大吃一惊。事后看来，青少年小说圈本身就是一个颇具启发意义的奇葩圈子，但对当时的她来说，这一切肯定既令人困惑又令人不快。

* 本周的“资本收益”栏目与往常略有不同：你永远不会真正体会到我们所有人都在以接近100%的效率运转，直到你无法再做到这一点[https://www.thediff.co/r/a342188e?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c]，而这一切的导火索正是一次突发的意外。 （昨天做了手术，一切顺利。）这提醒我们，我们已经习惯了各个层面上出人意料的高经济产出水平，而供应链（或肌腱）中的任何一个环节断裂都会极大地限制我们所能做的事情。

* 一位 Read.Haus 的读者询问，在什么情况下我们可能会面临计算资源过剩的问题 [https://www.thediff.co/r/1ca3cd47?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c]。简洁的回答是，这个问题越是带有反问性质，这种情况就越有可能发生。更详细地说：计算资源是一种棘手的产品，因为它可以替代很多其他东西：它可以替代许多不同类型的人力劳动，以不同的方式增强不同类型的人力劳动，而且，在用于构建提高工业流程和物流效率的算法的背景下，它还可以替代能源。就生成式广告而言，只要使用这项技术有利可图，大型广告平台的推理能力就能保持接近满负荷运转。但是，我们可能会高估经济增长的幅度，以及优化的空间——如果第一个万亿美元真的能帮助我们全面识别所有可能的产品或服务，第二个万亿美元能完美优化产品或服务的创建和分发，而第三个万亿美元却成了浪费呢？正因为这种极强的可替代性，观察哪些公司会遇到问题才更有意义。我们已经看到一些公司，比如xAI，购买了超出自身实际需求的计算资源，不得不将部分资源出售给其他公司。目前来看，这限制了收益。但这需要进行核算，而不是想当然。

Books

The Dictator's Handbook: Why Bad Behavior is Almost Always Good Politics [https://www.thediff.co/r/55a2432e?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c]: As a general rule, if two people recommend the same book to me over the course of a week, I will almost certainly read it. I really wanted to like The Dictator's Handbook, because it takes a realist approach to political outcomes—the actors aren't states, they're people, and the goals are personal, not ideological. I wouldn't say that this perfectly aligns with my worldview, but I would say that I apply exactly these kinds of considerations often, and find them fruitful. Countries, companies, and movements are all made of people, and even if they have some collective interest, that collective interest is an approximation of an average, not its own distinct thing.

《独裁者手册：为什么不良行为几乎总是好的政治策略》[https://www.thediff.co/r/55a2432e?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c]：一般来说，如果一周内有两个人向我推荐同一本书，我几乎肯定会读。我真的很想喜欢《独裁者手册》，因为它对政治结果采取了一种现实主义的视角——行动者不是国家，而是人；目标是个人的，而非意识形态的。我不会说这与我的世界观完全一致，但我可以说我经常运用类似的考量，并且发现它们很有成效。国家、公司和运动都是由人组成的，即使它们有一些集体利益，这种集体利益也只是平均值的近似值，而不是一个独立存在的事物。

I have three basic problems with the book:

 1. It has a model that feels a little more rigorous than it is. The high-level model is that the way power works is that there's a nominal electorate, who don't necessarily matter; there's a narrower selectorate, who actually decide who gets to be in charge; and there's an even smaller winning coalition, who expect rewards in exchange for their continued loyalty.
 2. It leans towards a model where most participants in the political system are trying to maximize their money. The book does acknowledge that there are some austere dictators out there, but still tends to assume that their inner circle is in it for the money. But, especially for a first generation of leaders, that's often not true! The shared experience of winning against all odds—the Long March, the October Revolution, Singapore surviving the withdrawal of the UK and the split with Malaysia, etc.—these kinds of experiences engender a deeper kind of loyalty than getting your percentage from Mister Five Percent whenever the government builds a bridge or licenses a mine. You can somewhat rescue the model here by saying that living up to one's ideology is a form of consumption, in which case Mao was the most profligate person of all time—it would take a staggering amount of spending to get that many people to agree to go through what he put them through.
 3. There is no polite way to say this, but: whenever the book talks about situations I'm not familiar with, like Samuel Doe's coup in Liberia, I learned a lot. And every time the book talked about topics with which I was more familiar, I felt like it was oversimplifying at best, and sometimes deliberately cherry-picking numbers to make a particular case. For example, when he's telling the story of Hewlett-Packard's entrenched management in the early 2000s, he compares their share price to the Dow. And, to be fair, HP was part of the Dow. But the period in question was uniquely bad for tech stocks, and money was rotating back into some of the old industrials. In the slice of time the book addresses, HP's shares fell 47%, compared to a 9% decline for the Dow. But the S&P dropped 18%, and the Nasdaq 100 dropped 35%. This doesn't change the thrust of the argument, but given that the book is built on what's supposed to be a systematic, rigorous, quantifiable theory, it's a bad sign that they'd fiddle with the data this way. In another case, the book compares two earthquakes in 2008, one in Chile and one in China, and ascribes the higher death toll in China to the Chinese government's weaker incentive to keep people happy. Maybe so! But, personally, if I were writing such a book, and using such an example, I would at least throw in a caveat about how China experienced the largest internal migration event in human history from countryside to cities over that time period, and that this affected both how high building standards were and how vigorously they were enforced. (Some of this could be mere laziness, not a deliberate effort to mislead; at one point the book uses the word "meretricious" in a context where it's clearly supposed to mean "meritocratic." Sloppy!)

1. 这本书的模型看起来比实际情况要严谨一些。其高层模型认为，权力运作的方式是这样的：存在一个名义上的选民群体，他们实际上并不重要；存在一个更小的遴选群体，他们真正决定谁能掌权；还有一个更小的获胜联盟，他们期望以持续的忠诚换取回报。

2. 这本书倾向于这样一种模型：政治体系中的大多数参与者都在努力最大化自己的利益。书中确实承认存在一些严苛的独裁者，但仍然倾向于假设他们的核心圈子都是为了金钱。然而，尤其对于第一代领导人来说，情况往往并非如此！共同经历的战胜逆境的胜利——长征、十月革命、新加坡在英国撤离和与马来西亚分裂后幸存下来等等——这些经历所培养的忠诚度，远比政府每次修桥或发放矿山许可证时从“百分之五先生”那里获得分成要深刻得多。你可以这样勉强挽救这个模型：践行自己的意识形态是一种消费，如果真是这样，毛泽东就是史上最挥霍无度的人——要让那么多人同意接受他所施加的一切，需要多么惊人的花费啊！

3. 恕我直言：每当书中谈到我不熟悉的情境，比如塞缪尔·多伊在利比里亚发动的政变，我都受益匪浅。而每当书中谈到我比较熟悉的话题，我都觉得它充其量是过于简化，有时甚至故意断章取义地引用数据来佐证某个观点。例如，在讲述惠普公司2000年代初期的管理层固守成规时，他将惠普的股价与道琼斯指数进行了比较。公平地说，惠普当时确实是道琼斯指数的成分股。但那段时期科技股的行情确实异常糟糕，资金开始回流到一些老牌工业股。在本书所探讨的时间段内，惠普股价下跌了47%，而道琼斯指数仅下跌了9%。但标普500指数下跌了18%，纳斯达克100指数下跌了35%。这并没有改变本书的论点，但考虑到本书旨在构建一套系统、严谨、可量化的理论，他们以这种方式篡改数据无疑是一个不好的迹象。书中还比较了2008年智利和中国发生的两场地震，并将中国地震死亡人数较高归因于中国政府维护民生的动力不足。也许是这样！但就我个人而言，如果是我来写这样一本书，并使用这样的例子，我至少会补充说明，当时中国正经历着人类历史上规模最大的农村人口向城市迁移的时期，而这必然会影响到建筑标准的高低以及执行力度。 （其中一些可能只是因为懒惰，并非故意误导；书中有一处使用了“meretricious”一词，而它显然应该是指“精英主义”。真是马虎！）

The book doesn't apply its model as consistently as I would prefer. For example, it talks a bit about how undemocratic the electoral college is, and also how gerrymandering reduces the share of the electorate needed to win. But then it gerrymanders its own description of this, by noting that in a parliamentary system, you only need enough votes to win half of the seats by one vote, or a little more than 25%. Which is only meaningfully true if you wait until the votes are cast and then draw the districts accordingly. If gerrymandering is bad, one shouldn't do it in text, either.

这本书对它的模型运用并不像我希望的那样始终如一。例如，它谈到选举团制度的不民主之处，以及选区划分如何降低赢得选举所需的选民比例。但它随后又对这一问题进行了自我修正，指出在议会制下，你只需要赢得半数席位（以一票之差），也就是略高于25%的选票即可。然而，只有在投票结束后再据此划分选区，这个说法才有意义。如果选区划分不好，那么在文字中也不应该出现这种情况。

But the book misses a more important point about elections, which is that there are many mediating factors between the will of the voters and the electoral outcome. For example, if you're an American, you recently had an opportunity to vote for either Donald Trump or Kamala Harris (or cast a protest vote, I guess). So, in a sense, the outcome was your choice. But, how did it come to be that those two people were your top two choices for who gets to control the nuclear codes? It's not like you made a list of all of your friends, ranked them based on various traits, and then said "I think Don's experience developing golf courses is pretty compelling, but my buddy Kamala worked as a lawyer, so I guess of all the people I know, I think probably one of them should be the most powerful person in the world." No, they got selected through two narrow-selectorate processes, that of political parties and of media attention. The book pays almost no attention to the media, other than mentioning Trump's efforts to restrict it, but in practice the media serve the same role that the electoral college originally did: you choose a small set of elites that you trust, and they will shape your worldview in a way that makes the right candidate clear. That's the only practical way to implement democracy; if we didn't have some system for narrowing things down to a small number of people who had institutional support, and actually asked everyone to just name who they personally thought should be in charge, you'd have a much wider distribution of votes, and also a completely dysfunctional system where the President would be a nobody to almost everybody. There's just no chance that 77 million people independently concluded that Trump should run things, and that 75 million of them thought that way about Harris. In essence, the presidential election is a plebiscite-veto where 75 million people said "I don't know who should be in charge, but it definitely should not be Donald Trump," and a few million more said the same thing about Kamala Harris.

但这本书忽略了选举中一个更重要的观点，那就是选民的意愿和选举结果之间存在许多中介因素。例如，如果你是美国人，你最近有机会投票给唐纳德·特朗普或卡马拉·哈里斯（或者投反对票，我想）。所以，从某种意义上说，选举结果是你自己的选择。但是，为什么这两个人会成为你心目中掌控核武器密码的首选呢？这并非是你列出所有朋友的名单，根据他们的各种特质对他们进行排名，然后说：“我认为唐纳德开发高尔夫球场的经验很有说服力，但我的朋友卡马拉是一名律师，所以我想，在我认识的所有人中，他们俩应该有一个人成为世界上最有权势的人。”不，他们是通过两个狭义的遴选过程选出来的：政党的遴选和媒体的关注。这本书几乎没有关注媒体，除了提及特朗普限制媒体的努力之外。但实际上，媒体扮演着与选举团最初相同的角色：你选择一小群你信任的精英，他们会塑造你的世界观，让你清楚地认识到谁才是合适的候选人。这是实现民主的唯一可行方式；如果我们没有某种机制将候选人范围缩小到少数拥有制度支持的人，而是让每个人直接说出他们个人认为谁应该掌权，那么选票的分布范围就会更广，而且整个体系也会彻底失灵，总统对几乎所有人来说都将形同虚设。7700万人不可能独立地得出特朗普应该执政的结论，而其中7500万人也不可能对哈里斯持同样的看法。本质上，总统选举是一场全民公投否决权，7500万人表示“我不知道谁应该执政，但绝对不能是唐纳德·特朗普”，另有几百万人对卡马拉·哈里斯也持同样的看法。

All that said, the book does make some informative and interesting points, and realists, even if they aren't so realist when talking about systems they participate in, they still make some great points. For example: it's hard for authoritarian systems to handle succession: you need everyone to coordinate on taking orders from the same new boss, but not to have that new boss kick out the old boss prematurely. So, among other things, this means that authoritarian regimes are very cagey about the health of their leaders. For authoritarian systems to survive for a long time, they need some kind of institutional norm that succession happens at a predictable pace, and that the successor is made clear in advance but also has clear limits on what they can do. The CCP had this for a while, though Xi Jinping's behavior shows that this kind of setup is fragile. The strongest part of the book covers dysfunctional former colonies. When they were ruled from afar, they suffered from the classic problem that there's a narrow group in charge, and they can afford to be indifferent to the plight of the average person. After those countries achieved independence, that was still how they were run: a small inner circle that was extracting wealth from the rest of the country, sometimes in horrific ways (the book contrasts the Battle of Mogadishu, in which the US to extraordinary lengths to rescue a hundred US soldiers, with the Battle of Afabet [https://www.thediff.co/r/f3006de4?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c], in which Ethiopian commanders, worried that their equipment would be captured, bombarded their own trapped soldiers in order to keep their equipment from being captured). So, I can paraphrase the apocryphal Gandhi line [https://www.thediff.co/r/7453cb29?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c]: my opinion of decolonization is that it

尽管如此，这本书确实提出了一些颇具启发性和趣味性的观点。现实主义者即便在谈论他们所处的体制时并非那么务实，也依然提出了一些极好的观点。例如：威权体制难以处理权力交接：既要确保所有人都能协调一致地服从同一位新领导的命令，又要避免新领导过早地罢免旧领导。因此，威权政权在领导人的健康状况上往往非常谨慎。威权体制要想长期存续，就需要某种制度规范，确保权力交接以可预测的速度进行，并提前明确继任者，同时对其权力加以明确的限制。中共曾一度拥有这样的制度，但习近平的行为表明，这种体制十分脆弱。本书最精彩的部分论述了那些运转失灵的前殖民地。当这些国家被远距离统治时，它们面临着一个经典的难题：权力集中在少数人手中，因此可以对普通民众的困境漠不关心。这些国家获得独立后，仍然沿用这种统治方式：一小撮核心成员从国家其他地区榨取财富，有时甚至以骇人听闻的方式（书中对比了摩加迪沙战役和阿法贝特战役[https://www.thediff.co/r/f3006de4?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c]，在阿法贝特战役中，埃塞俄比亚指挥官担心装备会被缴获，于是炮击被困的士兵，以防止装备被缴获）。所以，我可以转述一下甘地那句名言[https://www.thediff.co/r/7453cb29?m=5ad25eba-11f2-4b23-b354-f63de0d99a2c]：我对非殖民化的看法是，
